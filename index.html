Insurance From the Premium Up v1.1
Field guide / v1.1 · 20 October 2026, 5:43 PM ET · build 2026-10-20T17:43-04:00

An insurer sells promises now and pays for them later.

Customers pay first; claims arrive later, sometimes decades later. Paying valid claims in full is the product, not a cost the business hopes to avoid. The insurer's profit comes from pricing those promises accurately, spreading them across enough independent risks, and investing the money it holds in between. Almost everything in the industry, from the combined ratio to the catastrophe bond, is a way of pricing, financing or reporting that gap between cash in and cash out.

01  /  What it is

What an insurance company actually is

A manufacturer that sets its price before it knows its cost, then runs an investment fund with the difference.

Every other business learns its cost of goods before it sets a price, or at least soon after. An insurer does it the other way round. It collects a premium today for a promise whose cost will only be known when the policy period ends, the claims are reported, the lawyers finish, and the last check clears. For a homeowner's policy that might be eighteen months. For a medical malpractice or asbestos liability, it can be thirty years. Accountants call this the inverted production cycle, and it explains most of what makes the industry strange.

Strip away the products and an insurer does three things. It pools risks that are individually unpredictable but collectively stable, relying on the law of large numbers. It provides capital that absorbs the years when the pool is not stable, which is the part customers are really paying for when they buy catastrophe or liability cover. And it runs a claims operation, which is a service business with its own economics and its own reputation risk.

The income statement has two engines bolted together. The underwriting result is premiums earned minus losses, the cost of handling claims, and operating expenses. The investment result is whatever the insurer earns on the money it holds in the meantime. A company can lose money on every policy it writes and still report a profit if the investment engine is large enough and slow enough. That is the central fact of the business, and the rest of this guide follows from it.

02  /  The product families

Three industries that share a name

Property and casualty, life and annuities, and health insure different things on different clocks, and fail in different ways.

Property and casualty (P&C, called general or non-life insurance outside the US) covers things and liabilities: homes, cars, factories, ships, and the legal obligations that arise when someone is hurt. Within P&C the most important split is between short-tail lines, where claims are known quickly (a hailstorm dents a roof, the adjuster arrives next week), and long-tail lines, where claims emerge slowly and settle slowly (a product liability suit, a directors and officers claim, workers' compensation for a back injury that never heals).

Life and annuities covers how long people live. A life policy pays if you die too soon; an annuity pays as long as you live, so the insurer loses if you live too long. Modern annuity writers are less in the mortality business than in the spread business: they promise a fixed rate to policyholders and try to earn more on the assets. That turns them into something close to a credit fund, a point section 10 returns to.

Health insurance, particularly in the United States, looks like insurance on paper but behaves more like a payments and administration business. Federal medical loss ratio rules require insurers to spend roughly 80% to 85% of premiums on care depending on market segment, which caps the underwriting margin and pushes value toward pharmacy benefit managers, provider networks, and data. It is mostly outside the scope of this guide, and the Pharma guide in this series covers the drug side of that money flow.

03  /  The hidden product

Float is the product nobody advertises

Premiums arrive before claims leave. The money in between belongs to policyholders but is invested by the insurer.

Float is the pool of money an insurer holds that will eventually be paid out but has not been yet. It consists mainly of loss reserves (money set aside for claims incurred but not paid) and unearned premium (money collected for coverage that has not yet been provided). Float is a liability on the balance sheet, but the assets backing it sit in the insurer's investment portfolio and earn a return for shareholders.

The cleverest way to think about float is as borrowed money with a variable interest rate. The analogy is about economics, not intent: the claims are contractual obligations the insurer exists to pay, and the question is only what holding the money costs in the meantime. If the insurer writes business at a combined ratio below 100%, meaning claims and expenses are less than premiums, it is being paid to borrow. If the combined ratio is 104%, the underwriting loss is the interest cost of the float. Berkshire Hathaway's annual letters popularized this framing: its insurance float was roughly $170 billion at the end of 2024, per its annual report, and its long record of underwriting near or below breakeven means it has borrowed that money at a cost close to zero or less.

Long-tail lines generate more float per dollar of premium because the money sits longer before claims are paid. That is why long-tail casualty business is attractive in a high-rate environment and dangerous when reserves prove inadequate: the same delay that produces float also hides mistakes.

04  /  The one number

The combined ratio is the headline, and it is partly a guess

Losses plus expenses divided by premiums. Simple to state, easy to flatter.

The combined ratio adds the loss ratio (incurred losses and loss adjustment expenses divided by earned premium) to the expense ratio (underwriting expenses divided by written or earned premium, depending on convention). Below 100% means the policies made money before investment income. A 95% combined ratio on $1 billion of premium is a $50 million underwriting profit.

The trouble is the word incurred. Paid losses are facts. Incurred losses are paid losses plus the change in reserves, and reserves are estimates. When an insurer revisits last year's reserves and decides they were too high, the release flows through this year's combined ratio as a gain. When it decides they were too low, the strengthening flows through as a loss. A company can therefore report an excellent calendar-year combined ratio largely by releasing reserves set conservatively in earlier years.

Three versions of the same ratio

Calendar year

What the income statement shows

Current-year losses plus any changes to prior-year reserves. The number reported in headlines, and the one most affected by reserve releases.

Accident year

What this year's policies cost

Losses attributed to events that happened in a given year, regardless of when booked. Strips out prior-year development and shows current underwriting quality.

Ex-cat

What management prefers you look at

Accident-year ratio excluding catastrophe losses. Useful for trend, but catastrophes are not an accident of the business; they are the business for property writers.

Read with care

Favourable prior-year development, the industry phrase for reserve releases, can be a sign of prudent past reserving or a sign that today's earnings are being borrowed from yesterday's cushion. The tell is a trend: releases that grow while the accident-year ratio deteriorates.

05  /  The biggest estimate on the balance sheet

A reserve is a forecast booked as a liability

Case reserves for known claims, IBNR for claims that have happened but have not been reported, and an actuarial triangle to tie them together.

Loss reserves come in two kinds. Case reserves are set claim by claim by adjusters who know the facts. IBNR, short for incurred but not reported, is the actuary's estimate of claims that have already happened but have not reached the insurer yet, plus expected growth in known claims. For long-tail lines IBNR can exceed case reserves by a wide margin, which means the largest liability on the balance sheet is a statistical projection.

The standard tool is the loss development triangle. Each row is an accident year; each column shows cumulative losses at successive valuation dates. The ratios between columns, called age-to-age or link ratios, show how much a typical year's losses grow as claims mature. Applying historical link ratios to immature years projects their ultimate cost. This is the chain-ladder method, and every refinement in reserving starts from it.

Social inflation and the casualty problem

Link ratios assume the future develops like the past. In US casualty lines that assumption broke in the late 2010s. Larger jury verdicts, third-party litigation funding, attorney advertising, and erosion of tort reforms raised claim severity faster than general inflation, a trend the industry calls social inflation. Insurers that reserved accident years 2015 through 2019 using older development patterns spent the early 2020s strengthening those reserves, which is why casualty reinsurance stayed more cautious in 2026 even as property pricing fell sharply.

06  /  Who decides how much is enough

Regulators ask one question: can you pay the worst year?

Capital rules, rating agencies, and accounting regimes each answer it differently, and the strictest one binds.

Insurance regulation exists because the customer pays first and cannot easily check whether the promise will be kept. In the United States, regulation is done by the states under the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, coordinated by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC). Insurers file statutory accounts, which are more conservative than GAAP (acquisition costs are expensed immediately, some assets are not admitted), and are measured against risk-based capital formulas that trigger intervention as capital falls. State guaranty funds pay some claims of failed insurers, funded by assessments on the survivors.

The European Union's Solvency II regime asks insurers to hold enough capital to survive a one-in-two-hundred-year shock over twelve months, a 99.5% value-at-risk standard, calculated by a standard formula or an approved internal model. Bermuda's regime, which matters enormously for reinsurance, has been assessed by the EU as equivalent to Solvency II. Bermuda also introduced a 15% corporate income tax on large multinational groups effective 2025, ending its zero-tax era for the biggest carriers.

In practice, rating agencies often bind harder than regulators. A commercial insurer whose AM Best or S&P rating falls below A- loses access to much of the business it wants, because brokers and corporate buyers will not place risk with it. Capital management at most carriers is therefore aimed at the rating agency model first.

One ratio to remember

Premium-to-surplus measures leverage: annual net premium written divided by policyholder surplus (statutory equity). Around 1:1 is conservative for property writers exposed to catastrophes; higher ratios mean each point of combined ratio moves equity more.

07  /  Insurance for insurers

Insurers insure themselves, and the tower is where you see it

Reinsurance converts a volatile book into a stable one, at a price set by how much capital is chasing the risk.

An insurer that writes thousands of homes in Florida is fine in an ordinary year and insolvent in the wrong one. Reinsurance moves part of that risk to another balance sheet. The company buying protection is the cedent (it cedes risk); the reinsurer may in turn buy retrocession to protect itself.

There are two basic structures. In a quota share, the reinsurer takes a fixed percentage of every premium and every loss, and pays the cedent a ceding commission to cover acquisition costs. Quota shares provide capital relief and growth funding. In excess of loss, the reinsurer pays only when losses exceed a threshold. Catastrophe programs are built from excess-of-loss contracts stacked one above another into a tower. The cedent keeps the first losses (its retention); each slice above, called a layer in the trade, attaches at one level and exhausts at another, written as "$200 million excess of $100 million".

Pricing is quoted as rate on line: premium divided by the limit of cover. Low slices that are hit often carry high rates on line; high slices that pay only in rare disasters carry low ones. Contracts usually include a reinstatement, the right to restore the limit after a loss for an extra premium, which matters in years with more than one large event.

08  /  Where risk is traded

Lloyd's, Bermuda, and the brokers between them

A marketplace that is not an insurer, an island built by catastrophes, and intermediaries who see every price.

Lloyd's of London is not an insurance company. It is a market in which syndicates, each run by a managing agent and backed by capital providers, underwrite risks brought by brokers. Historically that capital came from wealthy individuals called Names who pledged their personal wealth without limit. Asbestos and pollution claims in the late 1980s and 1990s ruined many of them, and the 1996 reconstruction moved old liabilities into a run-off vehicle (Equitas) and opened Lloyd's to corporate capital, which now dominates. A central fund backs the market's promises, which is why a Lloyd's policy carries a single market-wide rating.

Bermuda became the world's catastrophe reinsurance center in waves, each following a disaster that drained traditional capacity: the class of 1992 and 1993 after Hurricane Andrew, the class of 2001 after the September 11 attacks, and the class of 2005 after Katrina, Rita and Wilma. Fresh capital arrived when prices were highest, and the island's regulatory speed and, until recently, tax position made it the natural home. Most catastrophe bonds and collateralized reinsurance vehicles are domiciled there.

Brokers sit between buyers and capital. The large global brokers place the majority of complex commercial and reinsurance business, publish the rate indices the market quotes, and earn commissions from both placement and analytics. Their position gives them a view of every price, which is valuable and has occasionally been abused: the 2004 investigation into contingent commissions by New York's attorney general changed how brokers disclose payments from insurers.

09  /  Capital markets as reinsurer

When the bond market became a reinsurer

A catastrophe bond lets an investor sell hurricane protection and earn a spread for it, fully collateralized.

A catastrophe bond works like this. A sponsor (an insurer, reinsurer, government pool or corporation) sets up a special purpose vehicle, usually in Bermuda. Investors buy notes from the vehicle, and their money is held in a collateral trust invested in short-dated Treasuries or money market funds. The sponsor pays a premium that becomes the investors' spread above the collateral yield. If a defined catastrophe happens, collateral is released to the sponsor and investors lose principal; if not, they get their money back at maturity, typically three to four years later.

What counts as "a defined catastrophe" is the trigger. An indemnity trigger pays on the sponsor's actual losses, which suits the sponsor but forces investors to trust its claims handling. An industry index trigger pays on estimated losses to the whole market. A parametric trigger pays on a physical measurement, such as wind speed at a location or earthquake magnitude within a box, and pays fast but may not match the sponsor's actual loss, a gap called basis risk.

Pension funds and dedicated insurance-linked securities managers buy cat bonds because their losses come from weather and earthquakes, which are largely uncorrelated with recessions and equity markets. The asset class also has its own risks: collateral can be trapped for months after an event while losses are estimated, and models that price a one-in-a-hundred-year event are only as good as their assumptions about building values and the hazards of a changing climate.

Neighbouring structures

Collateralized reinsurance is a private contract backed by cash rather than a rating. Sidecars are vehicles in which outside investors take a quota share of a reinsurer's book. Industry loss warranties pay when industry-wide losses exceed a level. Together with cat bonds they form "alternative capital", now a permanent part of the property catastrophe market rather than an alternative to it.

10  /  The convergence

Life insurers turned into credit funds, and the AI buildout noticed

An annuity is a long-dated, fixed-rate liability. Private credit is a long-dated, higher-yielding asset. The match was inevitable.

A fixed annuity is economically a loan from a retiree to an insurer: the retiree hands over a lump sum, the insurer promises a rate for years or for life. The insurer profits by investing at a higher yield than it credits. In the low-rate 2010s that spread was hard to earn in public bonds, so annuity writers moved into private investment-grade credit, asset-backed securities, and loans originated by affiliated asset managers.

The result is a set of firms where an alternative asset manager and an insurer are effectively one machine. Apollo merged with Athene in 2022. KKR owns Global Atlantic. Brookfield bought American Equity in 2024. Blackstone manages large portfolios for Corebridge and others. The insurer gathers long-dated liabilities; the manager originates assets to match them and earns fees on the way. Much of this business is reinsured to affiliated Bermuda vehicles, a practice regulators on both sides of the Atlantic have spent 2024 through 2026 scrutinizing for adequacy of capital and transparency of asset valuations.

This matters beyond insurance because insurer general accounts are among the largest buyers of the long-dated debt that finances data centers, fiber and power. When Meta financed its Hyperion campus in Louisiana in 2025, the roughly $27 billion of bonds were reportedly placed largely with institutional buyers led by Pimco, which is itself owned by the insurer Allianz. Investment-grade private placements and asset-backed deals secured by data center leases fit annuity liabilities neatly: long, fixed, and rated. The Circular Economy of AI Capital guide in this series maps those flows; the point here is that the retiree buying an annuity is, a few steps removed, a lender to the AI buildout.

The risk being watched

Annuities can be surrendered, sometimes with modest penalties. A spread business funded by surrenderable liabilities and invested in illiquid private credit has a liquidity mismatch that looks fine until rates spike or credit losses rise together. Supervisors' attention in 2026 centers on valuation of private assets and the use of offshore affiliated reinsurance.

11  /  Risks without a long history

Cyber, climate and AI: the risks that break the pooling model

Pooling works best when losses are largely independent. These three are correlated, which insurers manage with limits, exclusions, diversification and capital rather than by declining the risk.

The law of large numbers works best when losses are mostly independent; insurers still write correlated risks, but they price, limit and capitalize them differently. A thousand houses do not usually burn on the same day. But a thousand companies can be hit by the same piece of malware on the same morning, and a single software update can stop millions of computers at once. Cyber is an insurance market built around managing that correlation: war and state-backed attack exclusions (Lloyd's required standard wording from 2023 after litigation over the 2017 NotPetya attack), sub-limits for systemic events, and growing use of catastrophe bonds for cyber. Supply outran demand in 2026: brokers reported US cyber reinsurance rates down about 32% at the April renewals.

Climate shows up less as a single new peril than as more frequent mid-sized losses. Swiss Re attributes more than 80% of the long-term growth in weather-related insured losses to exposure growth, more and pricier buildings in harm's way, and notes that wildfires, severe convective storms and floods produced a record 92% of 2025's insured nat cat losses. The visible consequence is insurers withdrawing from or restricting new business in high-risk areas, pushing homeowners toward state-backed insurers of last resort such as California's FAIR Plan and Florida's Citizens.

AI creates two exposures. The first is silent AI risk: losses caused by AI systems that fall under existing professional liability, product liability or cyber policies never priced for them. The second is the physical buildout. A single AI campus can concentrate tens of billions of dollars of buildings, transformers and accelerators on one site, more than any one insurer will hold, so construction and property programs for these projects are syndicated across many carriers and reinsurers. Affirmative AI performance and liability covers exist but are young, small, and priced without loss history.

12  /  Red flags

The promotional playbook

Six patterns worth discounting when an insurer, insurtech or fund tells its story.

Earnings quality

Releases funding the headline

Calendar-year combined ratio improving while the accident-year ratio worsens. The cushion is being spent, not rebuilt.

Definitions

"Ex-cat" as the main metric

A property writer quoting results excluding catastrophes is quoting results excluding its business. Ask for the five-year average catastrophe load.

Growth

Fast growth in a soft market

Premium growing well above the market when rates are falling usually means the company is winning on price, terms, or both.

Structure

Thin capital behind the paper

Fronting carriers and managing general agents separate who writes risk from who bears it. In 2023 the collapse of the reinsurance platform Vesttoo, after fraudulent collateral was alleged, left cedents holding risk they thought was ceded.

Technology claims

"AI underwriting" without the loss ratio

Better pricing should show up as a better loss ratio, sustained across a cycle. Customer growth and app ratings are not underwriting results.

Complexity

Affiliated offshore reinsurance

Annuity blocks ceded to a sister company in a lighter regime can be sound. They can also move risk out of view. The disclosure to read is who holds the assets and how they are valued.

14  /  The current position

Where the market stands in October 2026

Capital is plentiful, catastrophe prices are falling fast, and the casualty side is still being careful.

Data as of 15 October 2026. Figures and events in this section were checked against the sources listed at the end of the page on or before that date. Treat this section as a dated snapshot; the explanatory sections are evergreen.

The property catastrophe market is in the steepest softening in a generation. Guy Carpenter's global property catastrophe rate-on-line index fell about 12% at the January 2026 renewals and was down about 16% after the June and July renewals, which Artemis reported as the biggest annual decline since the late 1990s. Its US index fell 14% through April, the sharpest drop since 2014. Howden's January report put risk-adjusted global property catastrophe rates down 14.7%. Despite the declines, brokers describe pricing as still above pre-2023 levels after a cumulative rise during the hard market.

The cause is supply. Strong 2025 earnings, a below-trend catastrophe year, and record catastrophe bond issuance swelled capacity; Gallagher Re estimated dedicated reinsurance capital at $648 billion at year-end 2025 against premium growth of just over 1%. Swiss Re estimated first-half 2026 insured natural catastrophe losses at about $42 billion, the lowest first half since 2020, while noting that the long-term trend implies about $148 billion for a normal full year and that a peak year could exceed $300 billion.

Two counterweights are visible. Casualty reinsurance renewals were described as broadly stable rather than soft, with continued caution on US liability exposure. And ongoing conflict in the Middle East kept continuity of cover for regional cedents a priority at the mid-year renewals. Market commentary in the autumn pointed to further property declines at January 2027 absent a major loss, with one bank forecast reported at up to 20%.

What would change the picture

A large US hurricane or earthquake, a major cyber accumulation event, or a credit shock in private assets held by annuity writers. The first two would reverse property and cyber pricing within one renewal; the third would test the life-and-credit convergence described in section 10.

15  /  The words, defined

The vocabulary

Filter by category or search. Insurance vocabulary is precise and often contractual: the same word can mean different things in a policy, a reinsurance treaty and an accounting statement.

Property and casualty Lines

Insurance of things and of legal liabilities: homes, cars, businesses and the claims that arise when someone is harmed. Called general or non-life insurance outside the US.

Short-tail line Lines

A line in which claims are reported and settled quickly, such as property or auto physical damage.

Long-tail line Lines

A line in which claims emerge and settle over many years, such as product liability, medical malpractice or workers' compensation.

Personal lines Lines

Policies sold to individuals: auto, homeowners, renters, personal umbrella.

Commercial lines Lines

Policies sold to businesses: property, general liability, workers' compensation, commercial auto and specialty covers.

Specialty lines Lines

Risks that need bespoke underwriting, such as marine, aviation, energy, political risk or directors and officers.

Excess and surplus lines Lines

Coverage written by non-admitted insurers for risks the standard market will not take, with freedom on rates and forms.

Workers' compensation Lines

Statutory cover for employees' medical costs and lost wages from workplace injury, typically long-tail.

General liability Lines

Cover for bodily injury and property damage the insured causes to others.

Directors and officers Lines

Liability cover protecting executives and boards against claims arising from their management decisions.

Professional liability Lines

Cover for negligence in professional services; also called errors and omissions.

Cyber insurance Lines

Cover for breach response, ransomware, liability and business interruption from cyber events.

Business interruption Lines

Cover for lost income when operations stop after an insured physical loss.

Occurrence policy Lines

A policy that responds to events occurring during its term, whenever the claim is made.

Claims-made policy Lines

A policy that responds to claims first made during its term, common in professional liability.

Deductible Lines

The amount the insured pays before the policy responds.

Policy limit Lines

The most a policy will pay, per occurrence or in aggregate.

Exclusion Lines

A policy provision that removes a cause of loss or type of damage from coverage.

Written premium Underwriting & reserving

Premium on policies issued in a period, regardless of how much coverage has yet been provided.

Earned premium Underwriting & reserving

The portion of written premium corresponding to coverage already provided; the denominator of most ratios.

Unearned premium Underwriting & reserving

Premium collected for coverage not yet provided, held as a liability.

Incurred losses and loss adjustment expenses divided by earned premium.

Expense ratio Underwriting & reserving

Underwriting expenses, including commissions, divided by premium.

Combined ratio Underwriting & reserving

Loss ratio plus expense ratio. Below 100% means an underwriting profit before investment income.

Accident year Underwriting & reserving

Grouping of losses by the year the insured event occurred.

Calendar year Underwriting & reserving

Grouping of losses by the year they are booked, including revisions to older years.

Loss adjustment expense Underwriting & reserving

The cost of investigating, defending and settling claims.

Case reserve Underwriting & reserving

An adjuster's estimate of what a specific reported claim will cost.

A table of cumulative losses by accident year and age, used to project ultimate losses.

The factor by which cumulative losses grow from one valuation age to the next; also called an age-to-age factor.

The total an accident year's claims are expected to cost when fully settled.

Adverse development Underwriting & reserving

An increase in prior-year loss estimates, booked as a current-period charge.

A reduction in prior-year loss estimates that increases current earnings; also called favourable development.

A jury award far above expected values, commonly defined as over $10 million.

The insurer's right, after paying a claim, to recover from the party that caused the loss.

The alternation of hard markets (rising prices, tighter terms) and soft markets (falling prices, looser terms).

Rate adequacy Underwriting & reserving

Whether current prices are expected to cover losses, expenses and a return on capital.

Ex-cat ratio Underwriting & reserving

A combined or loss ratio excluding catastrophe losses; useful for trend, misleading as a headline for property writers.

The expected annual cost of catastrophes built into pricing or reported results.

16  /  Takeaways

Twelve things worth keeping

The point

Insurance is the business of selling capital against bad years and paying claims when they come, financed in part by holding premiums until they are needed.

Underwriting decides whether that money is cheap. Reserving decides whether anyone can tell.

1

Price before cost

The inverted production cycle means every insurer's profit includes an estimate.

2

Two engines

Underwriting and investing are separate businesses that report as one.

3

Float works like borrowed money

Its economic cost is the underwriting loss; below 100% combined, it costs less than nothing. The claims themselves are owed in full.

4

Long tails hide mistakes

The delay that creates float also delays the discovery of bad pricing.

5

Read accident year

Calendar-year results mix this year's work with revisions to old estimates.

6

Reserves are forecasts

The biggest liability on the balance sheet is a statistical projection.

7

The strictest capital test binds

For commercial carriers that is usually the rating agency.

8

Towers show appetite

Where the cedent retains and where cover stops reveals what it fears.

9

Capital sets the cycle

Prices fall when capital floods in after good years, not when risk falls.

10

Cat bonds are permanent

Capital markets are a standing part of catastrophe reinsurance.

11

Annuities fund credit

Life insurers are among the largest lenders to infrastructure, including AI.

12

Correlation is the enemy

Cyber, climate and AI strain the independence pooling depends on.
